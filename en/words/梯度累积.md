Splitting a large batch into several small batches, running the forward and backward passes on them in turn, summing their gradients, and only then updating the parameters once. Accumulating $m$ small batches of size $b$ is equivalent to one [[gradient descent]] step with a batch of size $mb$; when the loss is averaged, the accumulated gradient must also be divided by $m$.

Note: It makes batches too large for memory trainable, since memory need only hold the activations of one small batch; the price is that the small batches run one after another, no faster than computing them at once. Exact equivalence also requires consistent normalization of the loss: averaged over tokens, it must be divided by the total number of tokens in all the small batches, not averaged over the per-batch averages. In training large models, a step's batch often holds millions of tokens, made up by data parallelism together with gradient accumulation.

Example: A GPU holds only 8 sequences at a time and the target batch is 64. After every 8 sequences the gradients are only accumulated; the parameters are updated after the 8th accumulation, equivalent to one step with a batch of 64.
