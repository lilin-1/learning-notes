A way of organizing hardware in which each step of a computation becomes a circuit of its own and data flows through the circuits in turn. The output of one layer passes through on-chip buffers straight into the circuit of the next, without being written back to external memory. All the circuits work at the same time on inputs that arrive one after another, like an assembly line. It stands in contrast to the way a [[graphics processing unit|GPU]] launches [[kernel|kernels]] one by one.

Note: It eliminates instruction fetching, kernel launches and round trips of intermediate results, in effect [[kernel fusion|fusing]] the whole model into one kernel, and its latency is stable and predictable. The price is that the chip must hold the circuits for all the steps at once. Large models have many layers and large weights that do not fit on one FPGA, so designs usually turn to a general matrix engine reused layer by layer, or split the model across several FPGAs.

Example: Suppose a model has 3 layers and each layer's circuit takes 10 microseconds per input. If each input passes through all three layers before the next is admitted, one input completes every 30 microseconds, with only one layer working at any moment. In a pipeline, the three layers work on three successive inputs at once: each input still takes 30 microseconds, but one completes every 10 microseconds, tripling the throughput.
