Replacing 16-bit floating-point weights or activations with representations of fewer bits, to reduce memory use and the amount read. Common choices are 8-bit or 4-bit integers: each group of values shares a scale factor $s$, stores $q = \mathrm{round}(x / s)$, and is restored approximately as $s \cdot q$ when used. Because [[prefill and decode|decoding]] is limited by memory bandwidth, halving the bits of the weights also halves the weights read per step; the price is a slight loss of accuracy.

Note: Weight quantization mainly relieves memory and bandwidth and does not necessarily reduce computation; the [[KV cache]] can be quantized too, which directly increases the number of concurrent requests that fit. Fewer bits mean larger errors, so low-bit quantization often gives smaller groups their own scale factors, or uses calibration data, to preserve accuracy.

Example: The weights $(0.12, -0.50, 0.33)$, quantized to 4-bit integers with $s = 0.5 / 7$, become $(2, -7, 5)$ and are restored as $(0.14, -0.50, 0.36)$. A 7-billion-parameter model stored in 4 bits takes about 3.5 GB, $1/4$ of its 16-bit size.
