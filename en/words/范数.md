A function that maps a vector to a nonnegative real number measuring its size, written $\|x\|$. For all vectors $x$, $y$ and every real number $a$: $\|x\| = 0$ if and only if $x = 0$; $\|ax\| = |a|\,\|x\|$; and $\|x + y\| \le \|x\| + \|y\|$. The most common are the $p$-norms:

$$\|x\|_p = \Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i|^p\Big)^{1/p},$$

where $x_i$ are the $n$ components of $x$. For $p = 2$ this is the ordinary length, and $\|x\|$ without a subscript usually means it; for $p = 1$ it is the sum of the absolute values of the components; as $p \to \infty$ it becomes the largest absolute value. The norm of a matrix is often computed by laying out all its entries as one vector.

Note: A norm turns a vector into a single number that can be compared or held to a threshold: the distance between two vectors is $\|x - y\|$, and training uses norms to limit the size of gradients and to penalize large parameters. Norms tend to grow with the number of components, so a $d$-dimensional vector is often measured by $\|x\|_2/\sqrt{d}$, the root mean square of its components. The three norms weigh components differently: the 2-norm lets large components dominate, the 1-norm treats every component alike, and the $\infty$-norm looks only at the largest.

Example: For $x = (3, -4)$, $\|x\|_1 = 7$, $\|x\|_2 = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$ and $\|x\|_\infty = 4$.
