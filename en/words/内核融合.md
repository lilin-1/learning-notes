Merging several consecutive operations into one [[kernel]]. Without fusion, each kernel writes its result back to [[memory hierarchy|GPU memory]] and the next kernel reads it out again. With fusion, intermediate results stay in registers or shared memory, the input is read once at the start and the output written once at the end, and fewer launches are needed.

Note: Fusion is most effective for memory-bound operations: elementwise operations, reductions and [[layer normalization]] have very low [[arithmetic intensity]] and spend almost all their time reading and writing, so each round trip saved makes them noticeably faster. The price is a dedicated kernel for every combination; compilers such as PyTorch's `torch.compile` can generate some of them automatically.

Example: Add a bias to $N$ 16-bit numbers and then apply an [[activation function]], ignoring the small bias. With two kernels, reading $x$, writing the intermediate result, reading it back and writing $y$ move $4 \times 2N$ bytes; fused, reading $x$ and writing $y$ move $2 \times 2N$ bytes, which roughly halves the time.
