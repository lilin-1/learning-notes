Not keeping all intermediate activations for [[backpropagation]], but obtaining them when needed by redoing part of the forward pass from saved checkpoints; also called gradient checkpointing. The forward pass stores only the activations at the start of each segment; when backpropagation reaches a segment, it first recomputes that segment's activations from its checkpoint and then computes the gradients.

Note: This trades computation for memory. During training, the memory for activations grows with the number of layers, the batch size and the sequence length, and often exceeds that of the parameters. With $n$ layers in $\sqrt{n}$ segments, only $\sqrt{n}$ checkpoints and the activations of one segment are stored, so memory grows as $\sqrt{n}$ instead of $n$. The price is about one extra forward pass; the backward pass costs about twice the forward, so total computation rises by about a third.

Example: A network of 100 layers stores the activations of all 100 layers without recomputation. With a checkpoint every 10 layers, it stores 10 checkpoints plus the 10 layers of one segment during recomputation, about 20 layers' worth, a fifth of the memory.
